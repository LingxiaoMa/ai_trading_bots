Bot design and parameterisation

A practical discussion of configuration choices, hypothesis space, dimensionality, evaluation cost and the resulting parameterisation.

Configuration and hypothesis space

The bot is configured as a 14-dimensional composite filter, with two parallel composite lines (HIGH and LOW). Each line uses three weighted moving-average components: SMA, LMA and EMA, plus a smoothing alpha parameter. This creates a hypothesis space that is expressive enough to model both short-term momentum and longer trend structure while still being tractable for metaheuristic search.

Dimensionality and practicality

  • 14 degrees of freedom: 7 parameters for HIGH and 7 for LOW.
  • Window lengths, filter weights and EMA alpha each contribute to the search space.
  • High/Low component separation reduces degenerate solutions and adds interpretability.
  • Constrained ranges keep the evaluation cost manageable during optimization.

Search space constraints

The design enforces short-term HIGH windows (2-40) and longer-term LOW windows (21-100). This prevents the optimizer from selecting illogical configurations where the 'fast' component is slower than the 'slow' component. It also acts as a regularizer, reducing overfitting risk while preserving enough flexibility to capture market structure.

Iteration and learning

  • Initial trials with fully unconstrained ranges produced inconsistent HIGH/LOW behaviour.
  • Introducing asymmetric window bounds improved signal stability and reduced noisy crossovers.
  • Empirical evaluation showed that narrower hypothesis spaces led to better out-of-sample resilience.
  • Practicality dictated a compact yet rich parameterisation that the metaheuristics could explore efficiently.

Resulting parameterisation

  • HIGH and LOW each combine SMA/LMA/EMA weights and duration controls.
  • EMA alpha is tuned alongside moving-average durations for adaptive smoothing.
  • Final hypotheses balance responsiveness with trend stability.
  • The 14-dimensional parameter set is well suited for CS, ALO, SA and RS search strategies.

Experiments and evaluation

Detailed training and validation setup, with experiment design and evaluation criteria for the trading agent.

Training protocol

  • Training on pre-2020 Bitcoin history, limiting overfitting to the chosen regime.
  • Fixed 9,000-evaluation budget per algorithm for fair comparison.
  • Single-segment and multi-segment fitness functions probe robustness.
  • High/Low signal component constraints improve filter consistency.

Validation strategy

  • Out-of-sample testing on 2020+ BTC prices with transaction cost model.
  • Buy-and-hold benchmark used to measure live-market resilience.
  • Interactive signal charts verify trade entry and exit quality.
  • Performance metrics focused on generalization rather than training peak only.

Experiment variables

  • Algorithm type: CS, ALO, SA, RS.
  • Signal design: 14 parameters controlling HIGH/LOW crossover filters.
  • Regime tests: single-segment vs multi-segment training objectives.
  • Evaluation focus: portfolio growth, convergence stability, and signal interpretability.

Conclusions

  • Design choice: A 14-dimensional composite signal allowed rich signal shaping while remaining computationally feasible.
  • Algorithm selection: CS and ALO were chosen to probe global search and swarm strategies; SA provided a local refinement baseline.
  • Experimentation: Iterating on parameter budgets and constraint settings highlighted trade-offs between evaluation cost and stability.
  • Result interpretation: Training success does not guarantee test robustness; live-market comparisons show buy-and-hold can outperform some metaheuristics in the selected period.
  • Academic takeaway: The project demonstrates how metaheuristic behavior must be assessed by both convergence and out-of-sample generalization.

Conclusions

Summarized findings highlighting generalization, algorithm selection, and practical implications.

Key findings

  • Buy-and-hold excels in the bullish 2020+ test window, but its robustness is regime-dependent.
  • SA often generalizes better than population-based methods when training overfits.
  • CS shows superior multi-segment robustness thanks to Lévy-flight diversity.
  • High/Low parameter constraints improved signal stability and reduced overfitting risk.

Practical implication

  • Out-of-sample evaluation is critical for algorithmic trading research.
  • Training performance alone is insufficient to select live trading strategies.
  • Algorithm comparison should include both convergence and generalization metrics.
  • Visual evidence from charts provides the necessary support for these conclusions.
