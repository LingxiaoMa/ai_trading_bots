## 1. Why All Algorithms Underperformed Buy & Hold, and Why B&H Is Not Robust

The superior performance of Buy & Hold (B&H) was determined primarily by the characteristics of the test dataset rather than by any inherent superiority of the strategy itself.

During the testing period (2020–2022), Bitcoin increased from approximately $7,200 to $43,000, representing nearly a six-fold rise within a strong long-term bullish trend. Under such market conditions, the optimal strategy is effectively to do nothing. Any sell signal generated by an active trading algorithm risks missing subsequent upward movements, while each trade additionally incurs a 3% transaction fee, placing active trading strategies at an inherent disadvantage.

However, B&H is not a robust strategy because its success depends heavily on the selected market regime. If the testing period were changed, the conclusion could be completely reversed. For example, during the 2018 bear market, Bitcoin fell from approximately $20,000 to $3,500, resulting in an 82% loss for B&H investors. In contrast, trading algorithms capable of identifying downward trends would significantly outperform B&H under such conditions. Therefore, the success of B&H in this experiment is largely due to the coincidental selection of a bullish testing period rather than any ability to adapt to varying market environments.

## 2. Why Simulated Annealing Outperformed Population-Based Algorithms in the Single-Segment Experiment

In the single-segment experiment, Simulated Annealing (SA) achieved a test result of approximately $4,776, outperforming Antlion Optimizer (ALO) at $2,773 and Cuckoo Search (CS) at $2,205. At first glance, this appears counterintuitive, as a single-state optimization algorithm would generally be expected to perform worse than population-based methods maintaining 30 candidate solutions.

The reason lies in the fact that SA’s weakness effectively became an advantage in this setting. The cooling rate of 0.98 caused the algorithm to reduce exploration rapidly and converge early to a “good enough” local optimum. Although this solution was less refined, it avoided excessively fitting the detailed fluctuations of the training dataset.

By comparison, the population-based algorithms performed much more aggressive optimization:

* ALO continuously concentrated its population around promising regions through roulette-wheel selection and boundary contraction. This allowed it to discover a highly specialized solution with a training value of approximately $53,092, but the resulting parameters were overly tailored to the training data, producing only $2,773 on the test set.
* CS employed Lévy Flights to perform broader exploration and achieved a training value of approximately $37,920, yet still exhibited substantial overfitting.

Fundamentally, this reflects the bias-variance trade-off. SA exhibited higher bias due to limited exploration but lower variance because it avoided overfitting. Under the single-segment setting, this unexpectedly resulted in superior generalization performance.

## 3. Why Population-Based Algorithms Outperformed SA in the Multi-Segment Experiment

The multi-segment fitness function fundamentally changed the optimization objective. Instead of maximizing performance on a single market regime, the optimizer was required to achieve stable average performance across four distinct market periods. This produced a substantially different fitness landscape and introduced new local optima within the parameter space.

The weakness of SA became apparent in this setting. As a single-state algorithm, once SA converges rapidly to one local optimum, it retains only a single candidate solution. Since the multi-segment fitness landscape differs significantly from the single-segment case, SA became trapped in a new local optimum without any mechanism for maintaining alternative candidates or verifying broader generalization capability. Consequently, its test performance declined from $4,776 to approximately $1,422.

Population-based algorithms possessed a structural advantage under these conditions because they continuously maintained 30 candidate solutions distributed across different regions of the parameter space. Even when guided by the multi-segment fitness objective, the population diversity allowed some candidate solutions to remain effective across both the full-sequence dataset and the unseen testing period. Since the final reported solution was selected from 30 evolving candidates, the probability of discovering parameters with stronger generalization ability became significantly higher.

## 4. Why Cuckoo Search Outperformed Antlion Optimizer

In the single-segment experiment, ALO slightly outperformed CS ($2,773 vs $2,205). However, under the more rigorous multi-segment evaluation, CS significantly surpassed ALO ($4,505 vs $2,046). Because the multi-segment setting provides a more reliable measure of robustness, CS can be considered the superior optimization algorithm overall.

The fundamental difference lies in how the two algorithms maintain population diversity.

ALO contains two simultaneous convergence pressures:

1. Roulette-wheel selection repeatedly pulls the population toward the current best solution.
2. Boundary contraction progressively shrinks the search space.

Under the noisy single-segment fitness landscape, these mechanisms caused severe overfitting to the training dataset. When applied to the more complex multi-segment landscape, ALO similarly overfitted to the averaged training objective without discovering parameters that genuinely generalized to unseen data.

In contrast, CS employs Lévy Flights, where step sizes follow a heavy-tailed probability distribution. Most search steps are small and perform local refinement, while occasional extremely large jumps enable effective global exploration. Mathematically, Lévy Flight behavior has been shown to be close to optimal for random search in uncertain environments. Combined with the nest replacement mechanism ((p_a = 0.25)), which continually injects new candidate solutions, CS preserves population diversity throughout the optimization process.

The multi-segment fitness function imposed constraints across four distinct market regimes, creating a substantially more complicated search landscape. Under these conditions, the global exploration capability of Lévy Flights became increasingly advantageous, ultimately allowing CS to discover parameters that were genuinely robust across different market environments.

This can be observed directly from the overfitting ratios:

| Algorithm           | Training Result | Test Result |
| ------------------- | --------------: | ----------: |
| ALO (Multi-Segment) |         $38,419 |      $2,046 |
| CS (Multi-Segment)  |         $28,530 |      $4,505 |

Although CS achieved a lower training score, its reduction in performance from training to testing was far smaller than that of ALO. This indicates that the Lévy Flight mechanism enabled CS to discover solutions with substantially stronger generalization capability rather than parameters specifically optimized for the training data alone.

## 5. Why Constraining the HIGH and LOW Parameter Ranges Improved Performance

An additional improvement in performance was observed after introducing constrained parameter ranges for the HIGH- and LOW-frequency components. Instead of allowing the optimizer to search the entire parameter space freely, the durations of the two components were restricted to different frequency regions:

HIGH component durations: 2–40
LOW component durations: 21–100

They control the moving-average window sizes used inside the HIGH and LOW components.The HIGH component was explicitly encouraged to behave as a short-term, high-frequency signal detector, while the LOW component was constrained to represent a smoother long-term trend component.
Without these constraints, the optimization algorithms could produce logically inconsistent solutions, such as assigning very large windows to the HIGH component or very small windows to the LOW component. Although such parameter combinations could occasionally fit the training data extremely well, they often captured noise or regime-specific fluctuations rather than meaningful market structure, leading to severe overfitting and poor out-of-sample generalization.

By separating the frequency ranges of the two components, the crossover signals became more stable and interpretable. The HIGH component remained sensitive to short-term market movements, while the LOW component continued to filter out high-frequency noise and represent broader market trends. This improved the quality of crossover events and reduced random signal generation caused by overlapping frequency behaviours.

From a machine learning perspective, this modification effectively reduced the size of the hypothesis space and acted as an implicit form of regularization. Although the constraints slightly reduced the flexibility of the optimizer, they substantially lowered variance and improved generalization performance on unseen market data.
